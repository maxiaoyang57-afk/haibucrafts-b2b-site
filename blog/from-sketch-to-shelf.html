OEM Development

From Sketch to Shelf

A practical custom-development path for turning a theme, drawing or packaging idea into a repeatable wholesale product.

HAIBU CRAFT Editorial TeamJuly 25, 20268 min read

Custom development is most effective when creativity and production control move together. A sketch or mood board may communicate the idea, but the project only becomes manufacturable after size, material, color, finish, quantity, packing and quality expectations are defined.

The following workflow helps buyers and suppliers move from inspiration to a product that can be sampled, approved, repeated and packed for sale.

Step 1: Build the buyer brief

The brief should explain what the product is for, who will use or buy it, and what must be distinctive. Include drawings, reference images, target dimensions, colors, finish, estimated quantity, launch date, packaging idea and destination market.

Mark which details are essential. A supplier can propose more useful solutions when it knows whether the priority is visual accuracy, lower MOQ, faster development, target cost or a particular retail presentation.

Step 2: Feasibility and route selection

The supplier reviews whether the idea can be achieved with an existing style, a modified style, a custom mix or a fully new component. These routes have different implications for tooling, sampling, MOQ, unit cost and lead time.

Common development routes:
  • Existing product: standard design and packing, usually the simplest route.
  • Color or mix adjustment: existing components reorganized into a buyer-specific palette or ratio.
  • Packaging customization: standard product with branded labels, cards, bags or boxes.
  • New design development: a new shape or construction requiring additional feasibility work and sample approval.

Step 3: Quotation with assumptions

A useful quotation states what is included: material, dimensions, pack count or weight, mix structure, packaging, MOQ and any development cost. Assumptions should be visible. Otherwise, two quotations that look similar may describe different products.

At this stage, unresolved items should be listed rather than hidden. Examples include final artwork, barcode format, warning text, carton marks or project-specific testing.

Step 4: Sample development

The sample is used to verify the design and the manufacturing route. The buyer should review it against the written brief, not only decide whether it “looks good.” Check dimensions, color, finish, proportions, pack count, mix ratio and practical use.

When revisions are required, describe the difference from the current sample. “Make it better” is difficult to execute; “reduce the pink pieces from approximately half of the mix to one third” is measurable.

Step 5: Freeze the approved specification

Before bulk production, identify the approved sample or images and create a final specification. Include artwork versions, labels, dimensions, colors, quantity basis, packaging and inspection criteria. All teams should work from this same version.

Step 6: Production and in-process checks

Production control should focus on the features most likely to vary. Depending on the item, this may include color, shape, surface condition, dimensions, component ratio, count, weight or seal quality. Early checks are valuable because correction is easier before the full order is packed.

Step 7: Final inspection and packing

The final review confirms that the finished order matches the approved specification. Packing checks should cover the inner pack, label, quantity, carton protection and shipping marks. Photos or inspection records can help the buyer understand shipment readiness.

Step 8: Preserve the project record

After shipment, keep the approved specification, artwork, label files and quality points together. This reduces uncertainty for repeat orders and makes it easier to develop related colors, seasonal editions or new pack sizes.
