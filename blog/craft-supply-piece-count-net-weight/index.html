Pieces, Grams and Bags: Reconcile Craft Supply Quantities Before Shipping

A bag is a container, a piece is a count and a gram is a weight. Write down which one is being quoted and which one you will check on arrival.

By HAIBUCRAFT Buyer Resources Updated October 17, 2026

Sourcing guidance for a project-specific discussion. Confirm the applicable steps and terms in writing with HAIBU. See our editorial policy.

Quantity disputes often begin with a shared word that means something different to each party. “One hundred” might describe loose pieces, packed bags or finished sets. A weight-based assortment may contain different numbers of pieces as the motif mix changes. Define the selling unit, its contents and the carton structure together, then use the same terms in the quotation, packing list and receiving record.

01

Separate four levels of quantity

Start with the component unit, such as pieces or grams. Next define the selling unit, such as a counted bag or a pouch of stated net weight. Then record how many selling units are placed in an inner pack and how many inner packs belong in a master carton. These levels should be visible in the order record rather than compressed into a phrase such as “one carton of charms.”

A product page showing a bag quantity describes that listing or packing reference; it should not be treated as an automatic MOQ or the final packing agreement. For a mixed order, copy the agreed unit beside every SKU. Do not let a spreadsheet total add a gram-based line to a piece-based line simply because both cells contain numbers.

02

Write the arithmetic before the purchase order

Use a conversion that can be followed in both directions: pieces per bag × bags per inner pack × inner packs per carton. Keep each factor beside its unit. If a line is sold by weight, use grams or kilograms consistently and keep net content separate from the total packed shipping weight.

For illustration only, 100 pieces per bag × 12 bags per inner pack × 4 inner packs would mean 48 bags and 4,800 pieces per carton. This is an arithmetic example, not a HAIBU packing specification. Replace every factor with the quantities confirmed for your order. A clear calculation also makes it easier to identify where a proposed pack change affects carton totals.

03

Do not convert a mixed bag into a guaranteed piece count

A weight-based bag can contain a different piece count when its components vary in size or weight. A change in the proportion of large charms and small fillers can change the count without changing net weight. If a kit requires a particular number of each motif, specify that count directly and ask for confirmation of a counted assortment.

Distinguish an overall quantity requirement from an assortment requirement. “100 pieces” does not by itself establish equal quantities of five designs or the presence of every design in each bag. Write down required motifs, target ratios, exclusions and whether variation is acceptable. Do not promise a fixed kit recipe from an assorted supply unless the pack composition has been agreed.

04

Choose a quantity check that suits the components

For counted packs, agree how selected packs will be opened and counted. For weight-based packs, define net content and how packaging weight will be excluded. Counting by weight can be useful operationally, but its reliability depends on the scale, the reference sample and variation in the components. Do not use one average piece weight for several visibly different motifs without evaluating whether it is appropriate.

NIST Handbook 133, Chapter 4, treats packages labeled by count and explains that excessive variation in item weights can require counting rather than weighing. It is a useful technical reference for the distinction, rather than an acceptance rule automatically incorporated into your order. Agree the applicable quantity-check method and acceptance criteria separately; legal metrology requirements depend on the market and package.

05

Keep gross and net figures in separate fields

The shipping weight of a carton includes components and packaging. It cannot be substituted for the net content of the individual selling units. Record bag or pouch tare when a weight check requires it, and avoid subtracting an assumed packaging weight copied from a different pack format. Labels, inserts, containers and protective materials can change the packed weight.

Ask for a packing list that connects carton identifiers to the SKU and selling-unit totals inside. For mixed cartons, list the contents by product rather than using only a general theme description. At receiving, compare the carton labels and physical contents with that list before combining loose stock or discarding packaging needed to investigate a discrepancy.

06

Make discrepancies easy to investigate

Record the order reference, SKU, carton mark, selling-unit count, check method and result. Photograph the unopened label and the contents of any pack being investigated. Keep the checked material separate while the issue is reviewed. A report saying “short bag” is much harder to resolve than a record showing the agreed unit, observed quantity and exact affected pack.

Before shipment, confirm who will review an exception and whether it calls for recounting, repacking or buyer approval. After receiving, retain the final reconciliation with the order record. A consistent quantity vocabulary across purchasing, packing and warehouse teams reduces avoidable confusion and gives both sides the same starting point when something needs clarification.
