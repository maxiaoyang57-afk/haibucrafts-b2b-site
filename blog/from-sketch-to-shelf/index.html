From Sketch to Shelf

A practical route for turning a theme, drawing or packaging idea into a repeatable wholesale product with a controlled specification.

HAIBU CRAFT Editorial TeamJuly 25, 20269 min read

Custom development works best when creativity and production control move together. A sketch or mood board may communicate the idea, but the project only becomes manufacturable after size, material, color, finish, quantity, packing and quality expectations are defined.

The purpose of a structured workflow is not to remove creativity. It is to protect the idea as it moves through quotation, sampling, production and packing. Each stage should produce one clear decision or approved reference.

Step 1: Build the buyer brief

The brief should explain what the product is for, who will buy or use it and what must be distinctive. Include drawings, reference images, target dimensions, colors, finish, estimated quantity, launch timing, packaging idea and destination market.

Mark the non-negotiable features. The exact hero shape may be essential while the supporting filler can be flexible. The target cost may matter more than a special finish. A supplier can propose a more useful route when the project priorities are visible.

Creative brief example

Pastel ocean DIY kit

Hero item: 25 mm translucent whale charm. Supporting items: shells, stars and blue-white clay slices. Pack: one hero charm plus a controlled 12 g mix. Packaging: resealable pouch with a branded card. Destination: United Kingdom. Essential feature: the whale shape and translucent finish. Flexible feature: exact shell design.

Step 2: Choose the development route

The supplier reviews whether the idea can be achieved with an existing product, a modified style, a custom mix or a fully new component. These routes have different implications for tooling, sampling, MOQ, unit cost and lead time.

Four common routes
  • Existing product: a standard design and standard packing format.
  • Color or assortment adjustment: existing components reorganized into a buyer-specific palette or ratio.
  • Packaging customization: an existing product combined with branded bags, cards, labels or boxes.
  • New design development: a new shape or construction requiring feasibility review and sample approval.

Choosing the simplest route that still protects the product idea often reduces risk. A new hero shape combined with existing supporting items can create a distinctive collection without custom-developing every component.

Step 3: Quote the product and the assumptions

A useful quotation states what is included: material, dimensions, pack count or weight, assortment structure, packaging, MOQ and development costs. Assumptions should be visible. Otherwise, two prices that look similar may describe different products.

Unresolved items should be listed rather than hidden. Examples include final artwork, barcode format, warning text, carton marks, exact color references or project-specific testing. The buyer can then see which decisions are still open.

Quotation example

Separate the cost layers

Product cost, custom mold or sample cost, branded card printing, retail packing, laboratory testing and freight should be identified separately when they follow different quantity or approval rules.

Step 4: Develop and evaluate the sample

The sample verifies both the visual idea and the manufacturing route. Review it against the written brief, not only by deciding whether it “looks good.” Check dimensions, color, finish, proportions, pack count, mix ratio and practical use.

When revisions are required, describe the difference from the current sample. “Make the pink lighter” is subjective; a color reference or approved comparison image is more useful. “Make the mix more balanced” should become a target ratio or minimum count.

Record sample rounds clearly. Sample A, Sample B and the final approved sample should not be confused during bulk production.

Step 5: Freeze the approved specification

Before bulk production, identify the approved sample or images and create a final specification. Include artwork versions, labels, dimensions, colors, quantity basis, mix ratio, packaging and inspection criteria.

All teams should work from the same version. A later design change must be documented and its effect on production, price and timing reviewed before it replaces the approved specification.

Final approval file
  • approved sample reference and photographs;
  • dimensions, material, color and finish;
  • component count, weight or mix ratio;
  • bag, label, card and carton artwork versions;
  • quality checks and acceptable tolerances;
  • order quantity and packing structure.

Steps 6 and 7: Produce, inspect and pack

Production control should focus on the features most likely to vary. Depending on the item, this may include color, shape, dimensions, surface condition, component ratio, count, weight or seal quality. Early checks are valuable because correction is easier before the full order is packed.

The final inspection confirms that the finished order matches the approved specification. Packing checks should cover the inner pack, label, quantity, carton protection and shipping marks. Photographs or inspection records can help the buyer understand shipment readiness.

When project-specific testing is required, the test sample and model description must match the approved product. Laboratory timing should be included in the project schedule rather than treated as an afterthought.

Step 8: Preserve the project record

After shipment, keep the approved specification, artwork, label files and quality points together. This reduces uncertainty for repeat orders and makes it easier to develop related colors, seasonal editions or new pack sizes.

A well-documented project becomes reusable business infrastructure. The next order can begin with confirmed information instead of reconstructing decisions from old messages.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need finished artwork before requesting a quote?

No. Reference images and a clear concept are enough for an initial feasibility discussion. Final artwork is needed before printing or production approval.

How many sample rounds are normal?

It depends on the complexity and clarity of the brief. A controlled revision list can reduce unnecessary rounds.

Can packaging be developed separately from the product?

Yes, but product dimensions, pack count and warnings affect packaging size and artwork. The two workstreams should be coordinated before final approval.
